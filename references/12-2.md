## 12. Editing MPE

### 12. Editing MPE

MIDI Polyphonic Expression (MPE) extends the MIDI specification so that parameter control data can be tied to single notes rather than applied across an entire MIDI channel at once. Because of this approach, MPE-capable devices are able to shape several parameters of each note live, opening the door to richer, more expressive playing.

Before Live can take in per-note expression coming from an MPE-capable MIDI controller, switch on MPE Mode within that controller's Link, Tempo & MIDI Settings.

Enabling MPE Mode for a MIDI Controller.

Keep in mind that once you pick a MIDI controller with MPE turned on as a track's input device, its channel input routing locks to “All Channels’’ and you cannot pick out single channels.

If you'd like additional guidance on handling MIDI controllers, consult the [MIDI and Key Remote Control](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/midi-and-key-remote-control/#midi-and-key-remote-control) chapter. After your controller is configured, it can drive the process of [record new MIDI clips](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/recording-new-clips/#recording-new-clips) that hold MPE data.

Through the Clip View’s Note Expression tab, for every note in a clip, you can inspect and adjust these five MPE dimensions: Pitch (per-note pitch bend); next comes Slide (per-note Y-Axis); after that, Pressure (Poly Aftertouch/MPE Pressure); plus the two velocity-based ones, Velocity and Release Velocity (Note Off Velocity). With these controls you can polish how recorded passages express themselves, or program polyphonic timbral movement for MPE-capable instruments.

Bear in mind that MPE data for notes is viewable and editable in every MIDI clip, no matter if those clips originated from an MPE-capable device or through [other methods](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/editing-midi/#creating-a-midi-clip). The sections that follow walk through inspecting and adjusting MPE data.

### 12.1 Viewing MPE Data

A MIDI Clip with MPE Data in the Clip View.

To inspect a MIDI clip's MPE data, start by opening Clip View through a double-click on the clip, then either select the Note Expression Tab or press the key command Alt3 (Win) / Option3 (Mac), which brings up the Expression Editors along the bottom edge of Clip View. Four out of the five MPE parameters each occupy a dedicated expression lane: Slide, Pressure, Velocity, and Release Velocity. Out of the box, only Slide and Pressure are visible. The fifth parameter, Pitch, has its envelopes drawn directly over the matching notes in the MIDI Note Editor.

You can reveal or conceal any expression lane using the lane selector toggle buttons on the left side. Just below those lane selector toggle buttons sits a triangular toggle button that reveals or conceals all the active lanes together.

These Buttons Toggle Visibility of Expression Lanes.

Whenever every expression lane selector is concealed or disabled, a press of the triangular toggle button brings up all the expression lanes together. Resizing each lane on its own is done through its split line. To resize every lane at the same time, drag the split line that sits between the lanes and the MIDI Note Editor.

Holding Alt (Win) / Option (Mac) as you click the triangular toggle button shows all the expression lanes. Hide those lanes again — whether through the triangular toggle button, or instead by a drag of the Expression Editor View split line — and the lane visibility toggles vanish along with them.

Per-note controller movement under MPE shows up on the MIDI track meters. Should per-note controller changes travel through a meter, that meter's bottommost dot glows blue.

### 12.2 Editing MPE Data

With the Note Expression tab showing, click a note inside the MIDI Note Editor (or click any one of that note's expression dimensions), and the note appears inside a see-through overlay. Its envelopes surface, together with whatever breakpoints already exist, letting you reshape the note’s Pitch, Slide, and Pressure envelopes, whereas markers handle changes to the note’s Velocity and Release Velocity values. Notes that aren't selected turn gray, and their expression envelopes fade.

Select a Note to View Its Expression Envelopes.

Once you've clicked the note or envelope you intend to work on, every expression breakpoint of the selected envelope, plus the line segments joining them, turns into a draggable object. A selection is created by clicking and dragging across the envelope’s background. The list below explains how MPE data editing operates:

- Click somewhere along a line segment and a fresh breakpoint appears at that spot.
- Click a breakpoint to remove it.
- So that breakpoint editing goes faster, expression values display whenever you make, hover above, or drag a breakpoint. Keep in mind that when you hover over or drag a selected line segment, the expression value displayed matches the breakpoint value sitting under the cursor’s present spot.
  A Breakpoint’s Expression Value.
- To reposition a breakpoint, click and drag it where you want. When the breakpoint you're dragging belongs to the active selection, every other breakpoint in that selection shifts along with it.
- Right-click any breakpoint and pick Edit Value out of the context menu. From there you can type a precise value into the editable field with your computer keyboard. When several breakpoints are selected, all of them move in a relative fashion. In the same way, new breakpoints can be placed at a precise value by right-clicking a preview breakpoint and selecting the Add Value command.
- To select a line segment, click just beside it (not on it) or hold Shift and click straight on the segment. While keeping the left mouse button pressed, drag the segment to wherever you want. Should the line segment you're dragging fall inside the active time selection, Live adds breakpoints at the edges of the selection and the whole segment shifts as a unit.
  To Move all Breakpoints Within the Selection, Drag Any One of Them.
- Within the Note Expression tab, the grid starts out switched off so you can edit more precisely. Bear in mind that this grid keeps its own settings, separate from the grid in the other tabs, and those settings are stored with the clip.
- Should you want it, turn the grid on through the Snap to Grid options menu entry or with the Ctrl4 (Win) / Cmd4 (Mac) shortcut. Once the grid is active, breakpoints and line segments lock onto time positions occupied by adjacent breakpoints. A breakpoint placed near a grid line snaps to that line on its own.
- As you move a line segment or breakpoint, hold Shift during the drag to confine the motion to just the horizontal or just the vertical axis.
- Keeping the Shift modifier held down while you drag vertically lets you fine-tune the breakpoint or line segment value with greater precision.
- A neighboring breakpoint can be deleted by dragging a breakpoint or line segment “over” it horizontally until it's gone.
- Hold Alt (Win) / Option (Mac) and drag a line segment to bend it into a curve. A double-click with Alt (Win) / Option (Mac) held restores the segment to a straight line.
  A Curved Envelope Segment.
- Apart from the Pitch expression envelope, the Slide, Pressure, Velocity and Release Velocity envelopes can be scaled proportionally over a note’s whole length, much like velocities are for several selected notes. The procedure: click somewhere outside the note’s region first, then float the mouse over the envelope you want. As soon as the envelope goes blue, click and drag upward or downward, and it scales to match. The same thing happens when you edit expression envelopes across multiple selected notes together.
- It's also possible to shift every selected breakpoint by the same amount instead of scaling them. For that, begin by clicking the envelope you want to change, then press CtrlA (Win) / CmdA (Mac) to grab all the breakpoints, and drag up or down with the mouse to raise or lower their value; a leftward or rightward drag slides every breakpoint horizontally as one group.
- Moving a note carries its expression envelopes along.
- Stretching a MIDI note via the MIDI stretch markers in the MIDI Editor, or with the ÷2 and x2 buttons in the Notes tab, stretches any per-note expression tied to that note as well.
- Hold Alt (Win) / Cmd (Mac) with the grid off and Pitch breakpoints snap to the closest semitone. The same applies to Pitch values while in Draw Mode. To override this, use those same shortcuts when the grid is on.
- Pitch envelopes vanish once Fold Mode is switched on in the Note Expression tab.
- While the Note Expression tab is showing, the Zoom to Clip Selection command or the Z key shortcut sets the zoom according to the pitch bend values held within the time selection.
- While the Note Expression tab is showing, the Clear All Envelopes entry found in the context menu of the MIDI Note Editor and the per-note expression lanes wipes every expression envelope from one or several selected notes.

### 12.3 Drawing Envelopes

Once Draw Mode is active, a click-and-drag lets you sketch an envelope by hand inside the Pitch, Slide and Pressure expression lanes.

You can switch Draw Mode on or off for MPE data through the Draw Mode Option in the Option menu, by clicking the Control Bar’s Draw Mode switch, or by tapping B, then clicking the envelope you'd like to work on. Holding B as you edit with the mouse flips Draw Mode on temporarily.

Drawing an Envelope.

Keeping the Shift modifier pressed during a vertical drag lets you tune a step's expression value with more precision.

Activate the grid — via the Snap to Grid options menu entry, or else the Ctrl4 (Win) / Cmd4 (Mac) shortcut — and each drawn step takes on the width of the grid currently displayed; you can then reshape these with several [handy shortcuts](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/arrangement-view/#using-the-editing-grid). For a brief grid-aligned draw while the grid is off, hold Alt (Win) / Cmd (Mac) as you draw.

### 12.4 MPE in Live’s Devices and on Push 2

A lot of Live devices handle MPE and ship with MPE presets that add fresh layers of interaction and playability to your sound. Thanks to the expressive range built into these devices, you can also make use of polyphonic aftertouch on Push 2.

### 12.5 MPE in External Plug-ins

Live also lets you reach and modulate the MPE data belonging to MPE-enabled plug-ins.

Whether a plug-in device’s MPE Mode is on or off gets stored as part of that device’s default configuration.

When a plug-in offers MIDI outs and has MPE turned on, it can send MPE out as well.

### 12.6 MPE/Multi-channel Settings

When you need a particular MPE configuration, an MPE/Multi-channel Settings dialog box is reachable from:

- The Ext. Instrument device.
- The I/O section of Live’s mixer.
- The context menu of an MPE-enabled plug-in.

Such settings come in handy for hardware synths that demand a certain MPE configuration, or for plug-ins that lack official MPE support yet still work with MPE controllers because they're multi-timbral.

### 12.6.1 Accessing the MPE/Multi-channel Settings Dialog

Within the Ext. Instrument device, pick your Routing Target from the MIDI To drop-down menu. Next choose MPE in the second drop-down, reopen the menu, and pick MPE Settings…

The MPE Settings in Ext. Instrument.

To reach these settings from the mixer's I/O section, confirm that the device you wish to control is picked in the MIDI To section of the MIDI track’s output. Within the Session or Arrangement Mixer, set the MIDI To drop-down menu to MPE; then reopen that drop-down menu and choose MPE Settings…

The MPE Settings in the I/O Section.

With MPE-enabled plug-ins, these settings live in the context menu of the device’s title bar.

The MPE Settings in a Plug-in Context Menu.

### 12.6.2 The MPE/Multi-Channel Settings Dialog

The MPE/Multi-channel Settings.

These settings let you:

- Configure the MPE zone and the span of note channels Live employs when it sends MPE out to an external MIDI device or plug-in.
- Select the upper or lower zone and number of note channels.
- Select multi-channel mode, which sets an arbitrary range of note channels.

Options exist for both the lower zone and the upper zone. Since a track outputs to only one zone at a time, you'll need two tracks to drive both zones.

Every zone requires a global channel (used for non-polyphonic controls). For the lower zone that global channel is Channel 1, while Channel 16 serves the upper zone. Beyond that, you can hand a span of the remaining MIDI channels to each zone (broadly speaking, the count of channels you give a zone corresponds to how much polyphony you want there). One sample zone configuration could put channels 1-11 in the lower zone and channels 12-16 in the upper zone.

Note: Live supports zones for MPE output only, which proves especially handy with hardware synths that call for a specific zone configuration.

You could also rely on these settings, for instance, to hook two MPE synths up to a single MIDI interface (once more, with one connected via the MIDI thru of the other), or to arrange a synth that drives two distinct sounds by assigning them to separate zones. Two MIDI tracks in Live, both routed to the same MIDI output device, can be set so that one track handles the lower zone and the other the upper zone.

## 13. Converting Audio to MIDI

### 13. Converting Audio to MIDI

While Live’s warping lets you handle audio files far more flexibly than older audio software does, several methods also exist for pulling musical content out of audio clips and dropping it into MIDI clips, which opens up extra creative avenues.

With an audio clip selected, four conversion commands show up in the Create Menu or in the clip's context menu.

Context Menu Commands For Converting Audio To MIDI.

### 13.1 Slice to New MIDI Track

This command breaks the audio into chunks, each mapped to an individual MIDI note. Slicing isn't like the Convert commands further down, because it never examines the _musical_ context of your source audio. Rather, it merely cuts the source audio into stretches of time without regard to what they contain. The [Drum Rack](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/instrument-drum-and-effect-racks/#drum-racks) makes an excellent home for sliced files, and the bulk of the configuration occurs on its own once you settle a few options:

The Slicing Dialog.

Choosing Slice to New MIDI track brings up a dialog box. It presents a list of slicing divisions plus a chooser for picking the Slicing Preset. With the top chooser you can slice across an assortment of beat resolutions, or based on the clip’s transients or Warp Markers. Because a Rack holds no more than 128 chains, Live blocks you from continuing whenever your selection would yield over 128 slices. The remedy is to drop to a coarser slice resolution or to slice a narrower stretch of the clip.

A handful of Ableton-supplied slicing templates sit in the Slicing Preset chooser, alongside any you've created yourself and stored in your User Library’s [default presets folder](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/working-with-instruments-and-effects/#default-presets).

When “Preserve warped timing” is on, the clip gets sliced so that any timing shifts produced by warping carry over. Turn that option off and the warping-driven changes won't show up in the sliced clip; instead the sliced result plays back like the original, “raw” audio.

After you've set your slicing options and pressed OK, several things take place:

1. A new MIDI track gets built, holding a MIDI clip. That clip carries one note per slice, laid out in a chromatic sequence.
2. A Drum Rack joins the freshly made track, with a single chain for each slice. Every chain is fired by one of the clip's notes and holds a Simpler loaded with its matching audio slice.
3. The Drum Rack’s Macro Controls arrive pre-mapped to handy parameters on the Simplers, governed by whatever the chosen slicing preset specifies. Within the factory Slicing presets, those cover basic envelope controls plus parameters that tweak each slice's loop and crossfade properties. Turning one Macro Control adjusts the linked parameter across all the Simplers at the same time.

Note: Live needs a brief moment to crunch through all of this data.

When you play the MIDI clip, it fires each Drum Rack chain in sequence, following the timing you set or the timing baked into the audio. That opens the door to plenty of fresh editing options, such as:

### 13.1.1 Resequencing Slices

Rearranging the Sliced MIDI Data.

Out of the box, your sliced MIDI data lays out a chromatically-rising “staircase“ shape so the right chains fire in their original sequence. Yet fresh patterns are easy to build by just [editing the MIDI notes](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/editing-midi/#editing-midi). A comparable result comes from dragging the Drum Rack’s pads onto one another to exchange their note mappings.

### 13.1.2 Using Effects on Slices

Since every slice sits in its own Drum Rack chain, treating single slices with their own audio effects is straightforward. Want to send several slices through one shared effect chain? Within the Drum Rack’s chain list, multi-select the relevant chains, then hit CtrlG (Win) / CmdG (Mac), which groups them into a dedicated nested Rack. After that, place the effects following this new sub-Rack.

For yet more inventive options, give inserting [MIDI effects](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/live-midi-effect-reference/#live-midi-effect-reference) ahead of the Drum Rack a try. The Arpeggiator and Random devices tend to deliver especially intriguing outcomes.

Drum loops are the usual target for slicing, though there's no need to stop with them. Try slicing audio drawn from other sources, including voices and ambient textures. Those same resequencing and reprocessing moves work on anything you slice — now and then with surprising payoffs.

### 13.2 Convert Harmony to New MIDI Track

This command detects the pitches within a polyphonic audio recording and deposits them into a clip on a new MIDI track. That track arrives preloaded with an [Instrument Rack](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/instrument-drum-and-effect-racks/#instrument-drum-and-effect-racks) producing a piano sound (which you're free to swap for a different instrument if you prefer).

Bear in mind that this command, like the rest of the Convert commands, parts ways with slicing because the resulting MIDI clip doesn't reproduce the original _sound_; rather it pulls out the _notes_ and uses them to drive a completely different sound.

The Convert Harmony command handles music from your library, but you can equally land strong results by generating MIDI from your own recordings of harmonic instruments like guitar or piano.

### 13.3 Convert Melody to New MIDI Track

This command detects the pitches in monophonic audio and drops them into a clip on a new MIDI track.

That track ships preloaded with an Instrument Rack that plays a synthesizer sound. With the Rack’s “Synth to Piano” Macro Control, you can morph this sound's timbre somewhere between an analog-style synth and an electric piano. The instrument was built to be flexible enough for a solid preview, yet you can naturally swap it for another instrument if you wish.

The Melody to MIDI Instrument Rack.

The Convert Melody command handles music from your library, and it also lets you record yourself singing, whistling, or playing a solo instrument like a guitar, then turn that recording into MIDI notes.

### 13.4 Convert Drums to New MIDI Track

This command pulls the rhythms out of unpitched, percussive audio and sets them into a clip on a new MIDI track. It also tries to recognize kick, snare and hihat sounds, placing them into the new clip so they trigger the right sounds in the preloaded Drum Rack.

Just as with the Convert Melody command, you can tweak the transient markers in the audio clip before converting to steer where notes land in the converted MIDI clip.

Convert Drums performs nicely on recorded breakbeats, and equally on your own recordings like beatboxing or tapping a surface.

### 13.5 Optimizing for Better Conversion Quality

The Convert commands can yield compelling results on existing recordings from your library, and just as well on material you've recorded yourself. As an example, you might record yourself singing, playing guitar, or even beatboxing, then use the Convert commands to make MIDI that serves as a springboard for new music.

To get the most precise results, here's what we suggest:

- Pick music with crisp attacks. Notes that fade in or “swell” might slip past the conversion process undetected.
- Stick to recordings of single, isolated instruments. The Convert Drums command, for one, does best on solo drum breaks; if other instruments are in the mix, their notes get picked up as well.
- Reach for uncompressed, high-quality audio files like .wav or .aiff. Lossy data formats like mp3 can lead to erratic conversions, unless those recordings sit at high bitrates.

To work out where one note ends and the next begins in the converted MIDI clip, Live reads the [transient markers](https://www.ableton.com/en/live-manual/12/audio-clips-tempo-and-warping/#transients-and-pseudo-warp-markers) in the source audio clip. That lets you “tune” the conversion's outcome by adding, shifting, or removing transient markers in the audio clip ahead of running any Convert command.

Even though each command targets a specific kind of musical material, the “wrong” command can occasionally hand you very intriguing results. Take Convert Harmony, which typically builds chords. Running it across a monophonic clip (a vocal recording, say) will frequently produce notes that the original audio never held. That can be a fine way to kick-start your creativity.
