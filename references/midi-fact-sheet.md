# MIDI Fact Sheet

### 39. MIDI Fact Sheet

Alongside our ongoing development of the audio engine, Ableton has invested extra time studying how Live handles MIDI timing and refining it wherever that proved warranted. This fact sheet exists so that users can grasp the challenges of building a dependable, precise MIDI setup driven by a computer, and so we can describe how Live tackles those challenges.

For anyone equipped with top-tier audio and MIDI gear, the timing matters covered in this chapter usually won't apply. Should you have already put time and budget into tuning these aspects of your studio, and you aren't running into MIDI timing trouble, this material is likely unnecessary for you.

### 39.1 Ideal MIDI Behavior

It helps to lay out a few shared terms and ideas before examining the way MIDI operates inside a digital audio workstation (DAW). Three separate MIDI use cases need to be supported by any DAW:

1. _Recording_ describes routing MIDI note and controller data out of a hardware unit (a MIDI keyboard, say) into a DAW where it gets stored. The perfect recording setup would log that arriving data with flawless timing relative to the song's timeline — with the same fidelity you'd expect from recording audio.
2. _Playback_ covers two linked situations in the DAW context. In one, MIDI note and controller data travels from the DAW out to a hardware unit like a synthesizer. In the other, the saved MIDI data is rendered into audio inside the machine, generated by a plug-in instrument such as the Operator synthesizer. For either case, the perfect playback setup would deliver a flawless copy of whatever was stored.
3. _Playthrough_ means routing MIDI note and controller data from a hardware unit (such as a MIDI keyboard) into the DAW and immediately routing it back out to either an outboard synthesizer or a plug-in instrument inside the DAW. The perfect playthrough setup would “feel“ every bit as precise and immediate as a real instrument like a piano.

### 39.2 MIDI Timing Problems

Computer-driven MIDI is messy in practice, with so many moving parts that the perfect setups outlined above simply can't be realized. Two core difficulties stand out:

1. _Latency_ describes a built-in, steady lag in a system. It poses a notable challenge in a DAW, since digital audio can't move into or out of an audio interface instantly — it has to be buffered instead. Yet even acoustic instruments show some latency; on a piano, for instance, a bit of time passes between pressing a key and the hammer mechanism striking the string. For performers, brief latency typically isn't troublesome, because musicians can normally shift the timing of their playing to offset any lag — provided that lag stays steady.
2. _Jitter_ describes erratic or random lag in a system. It can be especially troublesome inside a DAW, since the various subsystems (for instance MIDI, audio, and the interface) run as separate processes. Data frequently has to pass from one such process into another — say, while turning MIDI data into a plug-in‘s sound. Achieving jitter-free MIDI timing requires precise translation among the several clocks belonging to the system‘s parts — the MIDI interface, the audio interface, and the DAW on its own. How precise that translation turns out to be hinges on numerous things, among them the operating system and the driver design in play. Far more than latency does, jitter produces the sense that MIDI timing feels “sloppy“ or “loose.“

### 39.3 Live’s MIDI Solutions

Two central premises underpin Ableton‘s handling of MIDI timing:

1. Latency always beats jitter. Since latency stays steady and foreseeable, both machines and humans can cope with it far more readily.
2. When you record while using playthrough, what you'll want captured is what you _hear_ — even though, owing to latency, that lands a touch after what you _play_.

Live tackles the difficulties built into recording, playback, and playthrough so the MIDI timing stays snappy, precise, and dependably consistent. For Live to log arriving events at the right spots along a Live Set's timeline, it has to learn precisely when each event came in from the MIDI keyboard. Yet Live cannot take them in straight away — they have to pass first through the MIDI interface‘s drivers and the operating system. Live's fix is to have the interface drivers stamp each MIDI event with a _timestamp_ the moment they take it in, and those stamps travel to Live next to the event so Live can tell precisely where the events belong in the clip.

While playthrough is active, a DAW is forever handling events that ought to sound the instant they arrive, yet which unavoidably already happened a moment ago thanks to built-in latency and system lag. That forces a decision: ought events to sound the instant they come in (which may yield jitter whenever that instant lands while the system is loaded) or ought they to be held back (which introduces latency)? Ableton‘s pick is to introduce latency, since we hold that adapting to steady latency comes easier to users than coping with random jitter.

Whenever monitoring is switched on as you record, Live tacks an extra lag onto the event‘s timestamp, sized according to your audio hardware's buffer setting. That extra latency lets events land in the clip at the moment you _hear_ them — rather than the moment you _play_ them.

To play back hardware devices, Live likewise produces timestamps that it tries to hand off to the MIDI interface drivers for scheduling the outbound MIDI events. That said, Windows MME drivers have no way to handle timestamps, so for any devices relying on those drivers, Live takes care of scheduling outbound events on its own.

Should heavy system loads bring on audio dropouts, Live keeps taking in arriving MIDI events regardless. When dropouts strike, playthrough may suffer timing glitches and audio distortion, yet Live ought to still log the MIDI events into clips correctly. Afterward, once the system has bounced back from the dropouts, playing back those captured events ought to be precise.

### 39.4 Variables Outside of Live’s Control

By and large, timestamps make for a remarkably dependable way to manage MIDI event timing. Timestamps, however, only hold meaning for data that lives inside the computer itself. MIDI data beyond the computer can't draw on any of this, so timing data heading to or coming from outboard gear gets handled by that hardware the instant it lands, instead of following a schedule. On top of that, MIDI cables are _serial_, so they can only carry a single piece of data at any moment. What this means in practice is that several notes struck at once can't travel together over MIDI cables and have to go through one at a time instead. Where events are dense enough, this may give rise to MIDI timing trouble.

A further problem that can surface — especially with hardware synthesizers dating to MIDI's earliest era — is that the unit's _scan time_ might run at a fairly sluggish pace. Scan time means how frequently the synthesizer polls its own keyboard for input. When that pace runs too sluggish, jitter can creep in.

Naturally, whatever timing troubles exist at the hardware stage may compound as further pieces of equipment join the signal chain.

Even inside the computer, how precise timestamps are can swing considerably, hinging on how good the MIDI hardware is, mistakes in the driver code, and so on. Live has no choice but to take it on faith that any timestamps riding on arriving MIDI events are correct, and that outbound events will be treated properly by whatever external hardware receives them. Yet Live has no means of confirming either of those.

Tests and Results

The way we test how incoming MIDI events are timed is laid out in this diagram:

MIDI Input Test Configuration.

What comes out of a _MIDI Source_ (a keyboard, or another DAW running long streams of random MIDI events) goes into a zero-latency hardware _MIDI Splitter_. One branch of what the splitter puts out gets recorded as a fresh MIDI clip in Live. The remaining branch goes to a _MIDI-to-Audio Converter_. That unit turns the electrical signal coming from the MIDI source into plain audio noise. Since the unit never interprets the MIDI data, it carries out this conversion with zero-latency. What the converter puts out then gets recorded as a fresh audio clip in Live. Were the system perfect, every event within the MIDI clip would land at exactly the same moment as its matching event in the audio clip. The timing gap between the MIDI and audio events across the two clips can therefore be gauged to establish how accurate Live is.

To gauge MIDI behavior across a range of conditions, we ran the trials using three separate audio/MIDI combo interfaces sitting at various price points, every one of them from well-regarded makers. These interfaces will be called A, B, and C below. Every trial ran with a CPU load near 50% on machines running both macOS and Windows, at 44.1 as well as 96 kHz, and across three distinct audio buffer sizes, adding up to 36 separate test configurations in total.

Windows:

- Interface A: Jitter peaked at +/- 4 ms, though most of it clustered around +/- 1 ms.
- Interface B: Across the bulk of the trials, jitter topped out near +/- 3 or 4 ms. Running 96 kHz with a 1024 sample buffer produced a handful of events reaching +/- 5 ms of jitter. Running 44.1 kHz with a 512 sample buffer brought the odd event up to +/- 6 ms. Throughout, the bulk of the jitter sat at +/- 1 ms.
- Interface C: Through most of the trials, jitter peaked near +/- 5 ms. With 96 kHz and a 512 sample buffer, a few events landed somewhere between +/- 6 and 8 ms of jitter. With 44.1 kHz and a 1024 sample buffer, a few events climbed to jitter of +/- 10 ms. Throughout, the bulk of the jitter sat at +/- 1 ms.

macOS:

- Interface A: Running 44.1 kHz with a 1152 sample buffer, jitter spread out roughly evenly across the +/- 4 to 11 ms range. Every other trial peaked at +/- 5 ms of jitter. Throughout, the bulk of the jitter sat at +/- 1 ms.
- Interface B: Through most of the trials, jitter topped out near +/- 4 or 5 ms. Running 44.1 kHz with a 1152 sample buffer, the jitter spread fairly evenly across the +/- 2 to 11 ms range. Throughout, the bulk of the jitter sat at +/- 1 ms.
- Interface C: Throughout every trial, jitter peaked at +/- 1 ms, and the bulk of events showed none at all.

### 39.5 Tips for Achieving Optimal MIDI Performance

To assist users in getting the best MIDI behavior out of Live, we've put together a set of suggested habits and program settings.

- Keep your audio hardware's buffer sizes as small as it will allow, which holds latency down to a minimum. You'll find the audio buffer controls under the Audio tab of Live‘s Settings, and they differ according to which kind of hardware you‘re running.
- Pair a top-quality MIDI interface with the newest drivers available, so that MIDI timestamps get produced and handled as precisely as they can be.
- Do _not_ switch on track monitoring when you're recording MIDI while you listen straight to a hardware unit like an outboard synthesizer (rather than hearing that unit's audio routed through Live by way of the External Instrument device). In the same way, turn off track monitoring while recording MIDI data produced by some other MIDI unit (a drum machine, for instance). With monitoring switched on, Live introduces latency to counteract playthrough jitter. For that reason, only switch monitoring on when you're genuinely playing through.

### 39.6 Summary and Conclusions

We trust this clarifies a number of connected subjects:

- The built-in difficulties of computer-driven MIDI setups.
- The way we go about resolving them inside Live.
- Further variables that lie beyond our reach.

As we noted earlier, the surest route to resolving MIDI timing trouble in your studio is fitting it with the finest hardware parts on offer. Anyone using such parts should find that every software MIDI system runs with no perceptible problems. For those whose hardware is less than ideal, though, Live still delivers an added measure of precision by cutting down jitter, albeit at the cost of a slight bit of extra latency.
